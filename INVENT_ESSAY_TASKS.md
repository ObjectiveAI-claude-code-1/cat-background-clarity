1. Assess the simplicity of the background surrounding the cat. Examine the visual texture of every area of the image that is not the cat itself — the surfaces, spaces, and environment behind and around it. Determine whether the background is smooth, uniform, and visually quiet, like a solid-colored wall, a clear sky, or a softly blurred bokeh, or whether it is visually loud and assertive, filled with busy patterns, complex textures, bold prints, or cluttered surfaces. A background that would look boring and unremarkable on its own is doing its job — it recedes and stays out of the way. A background that would still look visually busy and interesting even if the cat were removed is competing for the viewer's attention. Score this quality higher when the background is calm and self-effacing, and lower when it is complex, textured, or visually demanding.

2. Identify and evaluate the discrete, attention-competing elements present in the image beyond the cat. Look for specific things that act as secondary focal points and pull the viewer's eye away from the cat — other animals, human faces, brightly colored objects, text overlays, watermarks, logos, toys, food, screens displaying content, or furniture with strong visual character. Consider both how many competing elements exist and how powerful each one is as an attention magnet. Faces and other animals are extraordinarily strong attractors. Text demands to be read. Bright, saturated colors pull the eye involuntarily. An image with no competing elements lets the cat reign unchallenged, while an image crowded with such elements turns the cat into one subject among many. Score this quality higher when the frame is free of competing elements, and lower when multiple or powerful distractions are present.

3. Determine whether the cat naturally and effortlessly emerges as the singular focal point of the image. This is the holistic, perceptual outcome of the first two qualities taken together — considering the state of the background and the presence or absence of competing elements, ask whether a viewer's eye would land on the cat immediately, without hesitation, without wandering, and without being pulled elsewhere first. A cat may still emerge strongly from a moderately busy background if it is visually distinct through contrast, lighting, or spatial separation. Conversely, a cat may fail to emerge even against a simple background if it blends into its surroundings or if a single powerful distraction dominates the scene. Score this quality higher when the cat unambiguously owns the image and the viewer's attention settles on it completely and effortlessly, and lower when the viewer's gaze fragments, bounces, or must search for the cat among visual noise.
